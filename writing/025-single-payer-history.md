
History

Single-payer healthcare proposals have been a part of American healthcare reform debates for decades. Early proposals for national healthcare date back at least to the Wagner-Murray-Dingell Bills of the 1940s. The [earliest bill](https://www.ssa.gov/history/corningchap3.html), introduced in 1943, proposed a mandatory national health insurance program as part of a broader expansion of Social Security.

A particularly important development occurred in 1970 and 1971, when Representative Martha Griffiths introduced H.R. 15779, the National Health Insurance Act, and Senator Ted Kennedy introduced S. 4297, the Health Security Act. The following year, Kennedy introduced [S. 3](https://www.congress.gov/bill/93rd-congress/senate-bill/3) and Griffiths, along with Representative James Corman, introduced [H.R. 22](https://www.congress.gov/bill/93rd-congress/house-bill/22). Together, these proposals became known as the Kennedy-Griffiths Health Security Act, which proposed a comprehensive national health insurance system covering Americans through a federally administered program financed through payroll taxes and general federal revenues. The Kennedy-Griffiths proposal reflects an early version of what would later be described as a single-payer health care system, which places the federal government at the center of financing and administering health coverage rather than relying primarily on private insurers.

The first major party platform calling for comprehensive health insurance covering all Americans was the [1972 Democratic Party platform](https://www.presidency.ucsb.edu/documents/1972-democratic-party-platform). The platform called for a federally financed and administered system of "universal National Health Insurance" that covers all Americans and provides them with comprehensive benefits such as preventive medicine and complete protection against catastrophic costs. Together with the Health Security Act of 1970, the 1972 platform reflected a broader push for universal health insurance during the 1970s due to concerns regarding rising medical costs. Advances in medical technology, increased utilization of health care services, and changes in health care financing following the expansion of public insurance programs such as Medicare and Medicaid were among the factors contributing to rising health expenditures.

During the early 1990s, lawmakers introduced legislation proposing universal healthcare coverage through publicly financed single-systems, including Senator Paul Wellstone’s [American Health Security Act of 1993](https://www.cbo.gov/sites/default/files/103rd-congress-1993-1994/reports/93doc07b.pdf). Though the Clinton administration ultimately pursued a different approach based on regulated competition among private insurers, single-payer proposals remained an alternative in the national healthcare reform debate.

In 2003, Representative John Conyers introduced the Expanded and Improved Medicare for All Act ([H.R 676](https://www.congress.gov/bill/108th-congress/house-bill/676)), which he subsequently introduced in each Congress through 2017. Though Democrats gained control of both houses of Congress following Barack Obama’s election in 2008, the administration did not incorporate single-payer proposals into their healthcare reform agenda, focusing instead on expanding coverage through the existing public-private insurance system through the Affordable Care Act. 

Single-payer healthcare gained renewed national attention during the 2016 presidential election, when Senator Bernie Sanders advocated for Medicare For All as part of his campaign platform. In 2017, Sanders introduced the Medicare for All Act of 2017 ([S. 1804](https://www.congress.gov/bill/115th-congress/senate-bill/1804)) proposing a national single-payer health insurance program covering all U.S. residents. In 2019, Representative Pramila Jayapal introduced the Medicare for All Act of 2019 ([H.R. 1384](https://www.congress.gov/bill/116th-congress/house-bill/1384)).

Amid this renewed congressional interest, the Congressional Budget Office (CBO) published “Key Design Components and Considerations for Establishing a Single-Payer Health Care System” in May 2019. Rather than evaluating a specific bill, the report examined the major policy considerations involved in establishing a single-payer system, including eligibility, covered benefits, provider payment rates, and financing mechanisms. In December 2020, CBO expanded on this research by publishing “How CBO Analyzes the Costs of Proposals for Single-Payer Health Care Systems”, which modeled five illustrative single-payer designs to estimate their potential fiscal and economic effects in 2030. However, CBO did not issue a formal cost score for Jayapal's H.R. 1384 or any other specific Medicare for All bill through these analyses.


CBO Analysis

The CBO modeled five single-payer healthcare systems based on Medicare's fee-for-service program to estimate their potential effects on the federal budget in 2030. The models differed in three key features, including payment rates for healthcare providers and prescription drug prices, patient cost-sharing requirements (such as copayments and other out-of-pocket expenses), and coverage of long-term services and supports. CBO did not evaluate or issue a formal cost estimate for any specific Medicare for All bill. Instead, its analysis examined the potential implications of different single-payer healthcare system designs, with the resulting estimates representing hypothetical scenarios rather than an official cost evaluation of specific legislation.

Under the modeled single-payer systems, the CBO estimates that national health expenditures in 2030 (projected at $6.6 trillion under current law) would change by between a $743 billion decrease and a $290 billion increase. The most consistent source of savings is reduced administrative spending by payers, a decrease of $406 to $416 billion across all five options. Lower provider payment rates would save an additional $22 billion to $533 billion, with the largest savings in the two options that set rates below the current all-payer average. The most important factor tending to increase national health expenditures is increased use of care, estimated at $206 billion to $718 billion.

The CBO also estimates that a single-payer system would substantially increase federal subsidies for health care in 2030 by $1.5 to $3.0 trillion. Nearly all current federal health subsidies, including Medicare, Medicaid, and the employer tax exclusion, would be redirected into the single-payer system. However, federal subsidies would still rise because the government would take on costs now borne by private insurers, employers, households, and states.

Under the modeled options, the single-payer system would finance 71% ($4.2 trillion) to 84% ($5.8 trillion) of national health expenditures in 2030. Medicare, CHIP, and most Medicaid spending would be absorbed into the single-payer system, private insurance would be virtually eliminated, and state spending on healthcare would decrease significantly. Additionally, health insurance coverage would become nearly universal, with nearly 99% of U.S. residents insured and out of pocket-spending would decline from $721 billion under current law to between $255 and $579 billion. The supply of health care would also increase, but CBO projects that demand would grow by more than supply, leaving more unmet demand than under current law. That imbalance would correspond to increased congestion in the health care system, including longer waits, delayed care, and forgone care.

1.) Delays. The law lets HHS give a state more time (a "good-faith exemption", which can run no later than Dec 31, 2028). Has any state received one? Which states have asked?

As of November 25th, 2026, the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services (CMS) have not granted a Section 71119 good-faith exemption to any state. Section 71119 establishes new Medicaid work and community engagement requirements for certain adults enrolled through the Medicaid expansion, generally requiring them to complete 80 hours of qualifying activities per month unless they qualify for an exemption. Qualifying activities include, but are not limited to, employment, education, and community service. The requirements are scheduled to take effect on January 1, 2027, though the law permits the federal government to grant temporary good-faith exemptions to states that have demonstrated difficulties in meeting the implementation deadline.

On November 13th, the CMS introduced an alternative, optional “hold harmless” transition period through June 30, 2027, for all states. Under this alternative, states would still be required to implement the work requirements and begin assessing individual compliance on January 1, 2027, but states can temporarily refrain from denying or terminating Medicaid coverage due to noncompliance with the new requirements. CMS has also invited states that had already submitted a good-faith effort exemption request to update their mitigation plans and opt into the hold-harmless period instead.

https://www.medicaid.gov/Federal-Policy-Guidance/Downloads/smd26006.pdf 

Several states have requested additional time to implement the requirements.

On October 1st, 2026, Oregon Governor Tina Kotek led a coalition of five governors in sending a letter to the Department of Health and Human Services (HHS) requesting more time to implement the new Medicaid requirements. Addressed to HHS Secretary Robert F. Kennedy, Jr., the letter was signed by the Governors of Oregon, California, Maine, New Mexico, and Virginia. The coalition expressed concerns that the states lacked sufficient time to make the necessary operational, technical, and administrative changes to build, test, and implement systems that comply with the Interim Final Rule (IFR) issued by CMS. The governors requested an implementation delay tied to demonstrated readiness, allowing states additional time to ensure their systems can reliably comply with the new requirements while protecting coverage for eligible Medicaid beneficiaries.

https://apps.oregon.gov/oregon-newsroom/OR/GOV/Posts/Post/governor-kotek-pushes-for-more-time-to-protect-medicaid-coverage 
https://www.washingtonpost.com/documents/9a4ecd9a-5741-4489-ba1b-6949d3c42f7e.pdf 

On October 28, 2026, California's Department of Health Care Services (DHCS) submitted a formal request for a temporary six-month good-faith exemption from implementing the Medicaid work and community engagement requirements established under Section 71119. The request sought to extend the state's implementation deadline from January 1 to July 1, 2027, to provide additional time to prepare its eligibility systems and administrative procedures.

https://www.dhcs.ca.gov/wp-content/uploads/2026/10/California-Good-Faith-Effort-Exemption-Request.pdf 

On November 19, 2026, a coalition of 19 states and the District of Columbia submitted a joint request to CMS for a six-month good-faith exemption from Medicaid work and community engagement requirements, seeking to extend the implementation deadline from January 1 to July 1, 2027. The signatories included California, Colorado, Delaware, Hawaii, Illinois, Maine, Maryland, Massachusetts, Michigan, Nevada, New Jersey, New Mexico, New York, North Carolina, Oregon, Pennsylvania, Rhode Island, Virginia, Washington, and the District of Columbia. The coalition argued that CMS's optional hold-harmless period is insufficient because states would still be required to implement the work requirements and assess beneficiary compliance beginning January 1, 2027.

https://www.beckershospitalreview.com/finance/19-states-push-for-6-month-delay-in-medicaid-work-requirement-implementation/

2.) Lawsuits. Besides Massachusetts v. Oz (decided Oct 29, 2026), what other cases challenge the work requirements or the rule that implements them? Who brought each, where, and what's its status?
On September 18, 2026, five Medicaid beneficiaries, several medical organizations, and the City of Columbus, Ohio, filed a lawsuit, Taylor et al. v. Kennedy et al., in the U.S. District Court for the District of Maryland. The plaintiffs argue that provisions of the interim final rule (IFR) issued by the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services (CMS) violate the Administrative Procedure Act (APA) by unlawfully narrowing the statutory definition of “medically frail individuals.” According to the plaintiffs, this narrower definition would result in an estimated 30% increase in the number of individuals who would lose Medicaid coverage for which they would otherwise qualify. The plaintiffs further contend that the rule imposes additional documentation and verification requirements that are not authorized by Congress. As of November 25, 2026, the case remains pending, with the federal defendants’ reply due on December 2, 2026.

https://healthlaw.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/Taylor-et-al.-v.-Kennedy-Jr.-et-al_Complaint.pdf 
https://litigationtracker.law.georgetown.edu/litigation/taylor-et-al-v-kennedy-jr-et-al/ 

On November 5, 2026, the attorney general of Oregon, Minnesota, New Mexico, and Washington filed a lawsuit, State of Oregon et al. v. Oz et al., in the U.S. District Court for the District of Oregon. Rather than challenging the federal regulations implementing Medicaid work requirements, the lawsuit challenges the constitutionality of the requirements themselves. The states argue that the requirements would fundamentally alter the nature of Medicaid expansion by transforming it from a program to provide healthcare coverage to a program that uses healthcare coverage as a means to incentivize work. Further, they contend that the requirements coerce states into implementing the new provisions by threatening to withhold existing federal Medicaid funding. The plaintiffs drew on the Supreme Court’s 2012 decision in NFIB v. Sebelius, which held that Congress could not compel states to participate in the ACA’s Medicaid expansion by threatening to withdraw their existing federal Medicaid funding. Thus, the plaintiffs contend that by unconstitutionally threatening to coerce states into implementing a fundamentally new program, Congress has exceeded its authority under the Spending Clause of the U.S. Constitution. As of November 25, 2026, the case remains pending, with the federal defendants' response to the motion for a preliminary injunction due on November 30, 2026.
